# Writing Systems of the Americas

[Main page](../index.md) > [Languages and culture](index.md) > Writing systems

TMTW contains several living writing traditions that either survived from pre-Columbian civilizations or developed from contact between Norse and Indigenous systems.

## Maya writing

Unlike in real history, Maya writing remains in everyday use in books, newspapers, education, and public life. Its modern form differs from the historical script.

When the united Maya state formed in the late sixteenth century, it adopted Classical Maya as its written language and standardized regional graphic forms. Variant signs were reduced.

After Spanish colonization in the eighteenth century, missionaries used Maya writing as a tool of evangelization. They selected one sign for each syllabic value and created a standardized missionary orthography. The royal court and aristocracy continued to use a more traditional system.

After Maya independence in the 1830s, the missionary standard and court tradition were combined into the National Maya script for mass education.

## North American runes

The runes brought by Norse settlers had too few letters to distinguish all sounds. Like real-world medieval runes, the North American system developed diacritics to separate different values.

It also developed conventions unlike the Latin alphabet. For example, syllable-final *n* can be written with a diacritic. The modern system is used primarily for Vinlandic.

## Mississippi scripts

The Mississippi scripts developed from runes during the fourteenth century. They diversified greatly during the political height of the Mississippi kingdoms around 1600.

Most remain alphabetic in principle but arrange consonants and vowels vertically inside compact syllable blocks, producing a visual appearance comparable to Maya glyph blocks. Regional versions differ considerably. Some enlarge verbal roots to distinguish them from affixes, while others include a limited number of logograms.

Mississippi-derived traditions influenced the Pueblo Script, Pueblo Syllabary, and the Lakota adaptation of runes.

## Mexica writing

Mexica writing survives mainly in ritual use and on public monuments. Ordinary administration and publishing normally use the Latin alphabet. A fuller description of its modern standardized form remains to be developed.

## Sacred Quipu script

The [Andean Federation](../countries/andean-federation.md) established the Sacred Quipu script in 1826. It was designed as a compromise between the Inti religious expectation that a restored Inca society would use quipu and the practical needs of a modern state. It is used alongside Latin-alphabet Quechua in religious and symbolic contexts.
