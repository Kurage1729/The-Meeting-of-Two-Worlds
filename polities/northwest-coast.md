## Overview

The United Kingdoms of the Northwest Coast is a union of coastal kingdoms of many languages, joined under a single High King. It formed in two stages: a loose union centered on the Nootka in the early eighteenth century, and a more centralized union led by the Chinook kingdoms in the 1770s. Since the 1880s it has been an autonomous constituent kingdom of the [Kingdom of Vinland](../countries/vinland.md), retaining its monarchy and extensive internal autonomy.

## Formation

### The Nootka union

In the first half of the eighteenth century, a loose union of kingdoms formed around the Nootka. It had no strong central government, but it created the habit of common diplomacy and trade among coastal kingdoms. 

### The Chinook union

In the 1770s, the arrival of European maritime traders and the pressure of the fur trade encouraged a more centralized union. It was led by the Chinook kingdoms, whose royal house became the High Kings and bound the other kings together.

The capital was placed at Kemkemelay, at the mouth of the Fraser River, for reasons of convenience and as a compromise with the Salish-speaking peoples. The official language was Chinook. (This was not the Chinook Jargon of the trade networks but the Chinook language itself, in a form close to the speech of the Chinook homeland.)

The union extended from the Chinook region through the Fraser River and Tsimshian territories. Haida kingdoms joined in the 1780s and 1790s. Russian expansion led the Tlingit clans to form a military alliance in the 1790s, and after the war with Russia in c. 1802–1804 the Tlingit kingdom formally joined.

## Modernization and the Latin alphabet

In the early nineteenth century, a High King launched a program of modernization intended to hold together a union of peoples who shared no language. Until then the region had no writing system of its own. Around 1830 the High King adopted the Latin alphabet, which allowed Chinook and the other languages of the union to be written and made administration.

The reform was controversial. Houses whose rank, names, and land rights depended on the potlatch feared that written records would make the witnessing and gift-giving of the potlatch meaningless.

## The Oregon War and its aftermath

The union allied with Vinland during the [Oregon War](../history/oregon-war.md) of 1861. The United States won the war, and the Chinook kingdoms and other territories south of the 48th parallel were ceded. The loss of the Chinook homeland was a severe shock: the High Kings' own dynasty and the region that had led the formation of the union were both gone. The remaining union became a Vinlandic protectorate.

During the 1860s the union was divided and unstable. Distrust of the High King's house, together with conflict between pro-Vinlandic and anti-Vinlandic factions, weakened central authority. The royal house nevertheless remained a symbol of the united coast.

## Incorporation into Vinland

The Alaska controversy of the 1880s revived tension between Vinland and the United States. In response to American policy, the kingdoms of the union acted together and formally joined the Kingdom of Vinland, preserving the monarchy and internal autonomy.

By this time the Salish language of the capital region had become the most widely used language in practice. Chinook was kept as a symbol of the unified Northwest Coast, and the custom of writing Chinook and Salish side by side was preserved.

In the twentieth century, the centralizing policies of the Vinlandic government provoked resentment, and demonstrations took place in Kemkemelay.

## The Chinook homeland and the royal towers

The royal tombs of the High Kings are tall cedar towers that developed from older traditions of tree burial. Those of the Chinook kings before the union, and of the first High King, stand in the Chinook homeland, now in the United States. From the High King who carried out the modernization onward, the towers stand around Kemkemelay.

A cadet branch of the High Kings' house remained in the Chinook homeland. Because the Chinook alone were too weak to be granted a Crowned State, this branch holds no royal title in the United States.

### The visit of 1932

Improved relations between the United States and Vinland, and the rising status of Indigenous peoples within the United States, made a visit by the High King to the Chinook homeland possible in 1932. It was the first time in seventy years that the royal house had returned. The High King prayed at the towers of his ancestors.
