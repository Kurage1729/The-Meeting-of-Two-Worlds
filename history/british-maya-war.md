# British–Maya War

[Main page](../index.md) > [History](index.md) > British–Maya War

The British–Maya War was a conflict between the Kingdom of the Maya and Britain fought in 1887–1888 over control of Belize. The war began as an attempt by the Maya government to recover territory that had remained under British control since the colonial period.

## Background

After the restoration of the Maya kingdom in 1831 and the unification of the Maya region in 1838, the government in Tiho increasingly regarded British control of Belize as an unfinished remnant of the colonial era. Relations with Britain remained tense throughout the nineteenth century, although British support and economic interests in Belize continued to influence Maya foreign policy.

By the 1880s, the Maya government had strengthened its military and administrative institutions. King Muwaan Jol increasingly advocated the recovery of Belize and regarded the continued British presence there as a threat to Maya sovereignty.

## War

In 1887, Muwaan Jol launched an offensive against British Belize. Maya forces advanced into the interior and occupied substantial areas, but were unable to establish control over the Caribbean coast. British naval power and fortified coastal positions prevented the Maya army from achieving a complete conquest.

The conflict also attracted international attention. Mexica president Porforio Savi supported the Maya cause under his policy of Indigenous Pan-Americanism. The United States, while unwilling to allow the conflict to escalate into a broader international confrontation, eventually intervened as a mediator.

Muwaan Jol was killed during the war. His death resulted in the accession of his son, K’an Chitam, in 1888.

## Peace settlement

K’an Chitam continued the war briefly before pursuing negotiations with Britain. Under American mediation, the two sides reached a settlement recognizing Maya sovereignty over Belize.

The settlement allowed Britain to retain substantial economic interests in the territory. British companies were granted long-term leases, guaranteed rights to timber extraction, and continued access to major ports. The arrangement therefore transferred political sovereignty to the Maya kingdom without immediately eliminating British economic influence.

The settlement was regarded as a major diplomatic victory for K’an Chitam. Belize became part of the Kingdom of the Maya, while British commercial interests remained deeply embedded in its economy.

## Legacy

The recovery of Belize greatly strengthened the legitimacy of the Maya monarchy. K’an Chitam's successful conclusion of the war became one of the central achievements associated with the beginning of his long reign.

The settlement also left a lasting British economic and cultural presence in Belize. English-speaking communities, British-owned companies, and Caribbean trade remained particularly influential in the region long after the transfer of sovereignty.
