# History of Maya

During mid-1100s, Eurasian diseases had reached Mayan region, accelerating the decline of Chichén Itzá. During the 1300s, horses spread throughout the region, followed by wheat in the 1400s. While these innovations had limited impact on the Maya lowlands, they proved highly advantageous in the Guatemalan Highlands. As a result, highland states such as the K'iche'—already ascendant in real history—became even more powerful in this timeline.

Around 1500, the Mexica Empire expanded into the Maya lowlands. The Maya, divided following the collapse of Mayapan, were unable to mount an effective resistance and were gradually incorporated into the empire.

Around 1520, the Mexica attempted to extend their rule into the Guatemalan Highlands. However, they were halted by the K'iche' hero Tecún Umán, allowing the highland kingdoms to preserve their independence.

By c. 1580, the K'iche' had established considerable influence over the Maya territories under Mexica rule. With support from Spain, which sought to weaken the Mexica Empire through proxy powers, the K'iche' conquered the lowlands and established the Unified Maya. Although founded by a highland dynasty, the new state was culturally dominated by the lowlands and adopted Classical Maya as its literary and ceremonial language.

In the early 1600s, the Unified Maya became increasingly identified with lowland Maya culture and transferred its capital to Nojpetén. This period marked the empire's zenith, as it expanded from eastern Oaxaca to the lands of the Lenca peoples. Spain, primarily concerned with securing the Acapulco Corridor and subduing the Mexica heartland, largely tolerated Maya expansion during this period.

Following the fall of Tenochtitlan in the 1695, Spain turned its attention toward the conquest of the Maya. In 1734, Nojpetén fell after a prolonged campaign, bringing an end to the Unified Maya and incorporating its territories into the Spanish colony of Indias Septentrionales.

In 1821, the Kingdom of Maya declared independence from Spain. The following year, it was annexed by Cacamatzin's Mexica state, triggering the Maya War of Independence.

In 1831, Maya achieved full independence. K'inich Bahlam was crowned Kaloomte'. However, the authority of the nobility remained strong, and the kingdom entered a prolonged period in which monarchs were frequently deposed and replaced.

In 1848, the Kaloomte' suppressed a republican uprising, marking the beginning of a gradual consolidation of royal authority.

In 1865, the Kaloomte'—inspired by political developments in Mexica—greatly reduced the powers of the nobility. While the reforms were popular among commoners, they also established the monarchy as the dominant political institution, no longer dependent upon aristocratic support.

[K'an Chitam](../people/kan_chitam.md) ascended to the throne in 1888. Initially sympathetic to Savi's Pan-Americanist movement in Mexica, he later distanced himself from Mexica as it embraced an increasingly Mexica-centric nationalism, instead pursuing closer ties with the United States.

In 1917, K'an Chitam entered the First World War on the side of the Entente. Following Mexica's defeat, Pan-Americanism rapidly declined in influence. Growing criticism of K'an Chitam's conservative rule culminated in the promulgation of a new constitution on 1922, which significantly limited the powers of the monarchy. These events are collectively known as the [6 B'en Revolution](../event/6ben_revolution.md).
